# Who this is for, and what they can actually do

The first two steps of Nation and Macalister's curriculum design model are
**environment analysis** and **needs analysis**, and they come before anything
else. This project skipped both and went straight to principles, then spent
eight documents there. That is why it produced a pile of good features rather
than a course: every method was chosen on its own merits and none against this
learner's situation.

Answered by the product owner, 26/08/2026.

## Environment

| Factor | Answer | What it forbids |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Device | Mostly phone, sometimes a computer | Nothing may require a keyboard to be usable |
| Setting | **Often somewhere he cannot speak aloud** | A speaking-only path is unusable much of the time |
| Voice | Can speak aloud sometimes | Shadowing and pronunciation work stay, but cannot be the only route |
| Time | **30 minutes a day**, and that is the honest figure | Everything below is sized to 30 minutes |

**The number that follows from 30 minutes.** Cambridge puts zero to B2 at
500–600 guided hours. Thirty minutes a day, every day, is about 182 hours a
year. So B2 is roughly **three years** at this rate.

That is worth stating plainly rather than hiding, because it decides what the
product owes: at three years, the thing that matters most is not the fastest
method — it is that nothing wastes a minute, and that the learner can see
movement often enough to still be here next year.

## Needs

All four skills, genuinely: reading technical documentation, understanding
video and meetings, writing messages, email and issues, and speaking in
conversation. This is not a narrow course that can drop a skill.

## The bottleneck, in his words

> **Biết từ nhưng không ghép thành câu.**
> Knows the words; cannot assemble them into sentences.

This is the single most useful sentence in this document, and it contradicts
what the project has been building.

It is **not** a vocabulary problem, and the product has been treating vocabulary
as the master variable — known-word counts, coverage percentages, a reading
surface that measures how many words are recognised. Recognition is not the
blocked step. **Production is.**

Nor is it a perception problem, which is what the pronunciation work addresses.
Hearing /p/ apart from /b/ does not help someone who cannot build a clause.

### What the evidence says to do about exactly this

Formulaic sequences — prefabricated multi-word chunks retrieved whole rather
than assembled — significantly predicted speech fluency (β = .40, R² = .16), and
the mechanism is specific: they reduced **pausing**, and did not raise speech
rate. Pauses are where assembly happens. A learner who has to build every clause
from single words pauses; one who has *"I'm not sure whether…"* stored whole does
not.

That is the same finding read from the other side: the block is assembly, and
chunks remove the need to assemble.

## What this changes

1. **Production moves ahead of more input.** The reading surface stays — it is
   how words and chunks are met at all — but a session that only reads does not
   address the stated block.
2. **Writing production carries more weight than speaking production**, purely
   on environment: he is often somewhere he cannot speak. Writing reaches the
   same production system and works everywhere. Speaking work stays for when he
   can.
3. **Chunks, not words, are the unit to teach and to review.** The curriculum
   schema already calls its unit `targetChunks` and the review key is a word.
   That gap is now a defect with a reason behind it.
4. **Thirty minutes is the design budget.** A session that needs forty is a
   session that gets abandoned, and every screen should be reachable in that
   window.

## What is still missing from the model

Environment and needs are now answered. **Evaluation** — does this course
actually work for this learner — has no answer and no instrument beyond the
elicited-imitation measure at `/measure`. It stays owed, and it is the step that
closes the loop.
